Your AI should answer to you. Only you.

AI agents are starting to act with your data, your keys, your money and your name, on systems someone else controls. Sanctuary is the open-source ground they stand on: keys that stay on your machine, encrypted storage for what it keeps through Sanctuary, connection rules you sign, a human yes for the actions you choose, and a record you can check.

The problem

One bad web page can turn your agent against you.

Say you give an assistant your email, your files, your logins and a card for paying bills. It answers mail, sorts documents and pays what is due while you get on with your day.

Then it opens a page someone wrote for it, with instructions hidden in the text: send these files here, use the card, say nothing. The agent cannot tell those words from yours, so it does what they say, with everything you trusted it to hold.

A safeguard inside the agent holds only while the agent stays on your side. A safeguard run by some company asks you to trust that company. Sanctuary puts yours on your own machine, below the agent, where it does not depend on the agent's cooperation.

The answer

Four promises, kept on your machine.

  • Keys

    You hold the keys.

    Your agent's keys are made on your machine and locked under your passphrase, and the project keeps no copy.

  • Memory

    You keep the memory.

    What your agent stores through Sanctuary is encrypted at rest on your machine, readable only with your keys. Files it writes by other means are outside that.

  • Connections

    You decide where it connects.

    With the wall armed on macOS or Ubuntu 24.04, a policy you signed allows or refuses each destination for the agent's own account. Actions you mark for approval wait for your yes.

  • Record

    You can check what happened.

    Sanctuary writes what your agent does through it into a hash-chained record. Checking that record for tampering is still rated partial.

Where these stop today

The connection rules cover a separate account you create for the agent, and decide where it may connect, not what an allowed connection carries. The approval step covers only tool calls that go through Sanctuary, so a harness's own built-in tools skip it, and whatever the agent sends to its AI model can be seen by that model's provider.

See it

You stay in the loop, and you can check the record.

Actions you mark for approval wait for your yes.

When an agent asks, through Sanctuary, to do something your policy marks for approval, Sanctuary holds it and shows you what it wants and why it stopped. You allow it once or deny it, and the decision is recorded.

Check the record yourself.

Each entry is chained to the one before it, and the console checks the chain and reports what it finds. This check is still rated partial; the evidence page says what it covers.

Why it is different

It sits beneath the agent, on ground you own.

Below the agent
On macOS and Ubuntu 24.04, with the wall armed, your operating system enforces the connection rules underneath the agent, so they do not depend on the agent's cooperation.
On your own machine
It runs on hardware you control. There is nothing to sign up for and no account with us.
No vendor in the middle
No company holds your keys or a copy of what you store, the author included.
Open source
Every line is public under Apache-2.0, so you, or anyone you trust, can read what it does.

Where it runs

Keys, approvals and the record run on macOS and Linux. The connection rules are proven on macOS and on Ubuntu 24.04 (x86-64). Windows is not yet supported.

The macOS proof covers one host and one OS version: a signed policy that allows and denies per agent account and survives attended reboot cycles, not a per-rule, per-flow log; unattended reboot survival is unproven. On Ubuntu the connection rules come as the separate Castle Wall Linux 0.1.0-1 pre-release, proven on fresh installs with a test program; real agent harnesses, injected faults and some rule forms are not yet proven there. The record's tamper check, checking it from another machine, and taking your data with you are still partial. The evidence page shows what is proven, on which build, and what is not.

Install

Shown for Claude Code. It needs Node.js 22 or newer.

Terminal
npx -y @sanctuary-framework/mcp-server protect --claude-code --no-open --no-dashboard

This sets up your keys, the approval step and the record. Turning on the connection rules is a separate step on macOS and Ubuntu, and uninstalling keeps your data.
